The question

How do the money-literacy limitations students face shape the way they actually handle their finances, and what does that mean for anyone designing a tool for them?

The brief was exploratory: a financial management concept for students was being scoped, and nobody on the team could say with any confidence whether students avoided budgeting tools because the tools were hard, because they had no time, or because they saw no reason to bother. Those three explanations imply three completely different products.

I chose a survey because the disagreement was about distribution, meaning how common each reason was, rather than about depth. A handful of interviews would have given me richer stories and no way to weigh them.

IN HINDSIGHTThe reasoning was right and the execution didn't match it. If the question is about distribution, a sample of ten cannot answer it. I should have either widened the sample or accepted that this was a qualitative study and designed it as one.

The instrument

Fourteen items, self-administered, roughly five minutes. I wrote it to move from stable facts through behaviour to preference, keeping the two open-ended items last so that fatigue would cost me the least important data.

What the ten answers show

Counts, not percentages. With ten respondents a percentage is a rounding artefact dressed up as a finding. “55%” of this sample is five and a half people, and reporting it that way would imply a precision the data cannot carry.

Two context items are worth stating flatly: nine of the ten had never taken any course on managing money, and seven reported feeling stressed about their finances often or always. Self-report, single item, no validated scale, so directional at best, but consistent with what the open answers said.

What I got wrong

Three defects were built into the questionnaire before a single response came in. I found them while cleaning the data, which is later than I should have.

Item 3 offered “Other” twice

The income question shipped with two separate options both labelled as other, one of them free-text. Three of ten answers landed in one or the other, and a fourth respondent typed a combined answer, “part-time job and student loan”, that a single-select item had no way to hold. Income is therefore unusable as a variable in this dataset. A second reader on the draft would have caught it in a minute.

Item 6 was built as a ranking, so it measures nothing

I wanted to know which financial concepts students find hardest. The item forced every respondent to order all six, so every concept registers for all ten and the counts come out flat at ten across the board. The output looks like data and carries no signal. The right instrument was a pick-your-top-three, or a MaxDiff design if I wanted a genuine preference ordering.

The 5-point knowledge scale had no anchors

Item 4 asked students to rate their understanding from very poor to excellent with no definition of any point. Four said poor and four said fair, but there is no reason to believe two respondents meant the same thing by “fair”, and no way to check. A behavioural proxy, such as asking whether they can name what they spent on groceries last month, would have been harder to write and worth far more.

And the sample was whatever answered that day

One evening, one network, self-selecting. Median age 29.5, which is old for a student sample and tells you the recruitment reached my own cohort rather than students in general. Five of ten were international students, which suited the eventual design focus but was luck, not sampling.

WHY THIS IS HEREThese four paragraphs are the reason the study is worth showing. An instrument I can't critique is one I can't improve.

How I'd design it now

  • Define the constructs before writing items. Financial confidence, tracking behaviour and tool friction are three different things; this questionnaire moved between them without saying so.
  • Anchor every scale, or replace it with a behavioural measure. Frequency items with concrete referents, such as “in the past 30 days, how many times”, beat unanchored adjectives.
  • Use MaxDiff for the feature preference item. Items 10 and 13 both ask about desirability, and multi-select lets a respondent want everything. Forced trade-offs would have produced a real ordering.
  • Set the sample against the analysis, not the deadline. To compare international, EU/EEA and Dutch students I need quotas and a base near 100, not whoever replies before midnight.
  • Pilot with five people and a second reader. Both structural defects above would have surfaced in half an hour.
  • Decide the analysis before fielding. Naming the tests in advance is what stops a survey from quietly becoming a thematic exercise after the fact, which is what happened here.

The study did its job as exploratory input: it moved the team off “students don't see the need” and onto usability, and it killed gamification early. It was never strong enough to settle the question it was written to answer, and I'd rather say that plainly than round ten people up to a percentage.

WHAT CARRIED FORWARDUsability-first framing and the rejection of reward mechanics both survived into the concept that followed.
